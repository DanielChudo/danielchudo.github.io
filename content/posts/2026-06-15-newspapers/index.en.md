If we could transport a person from 1926 to our 2026, the first thing that would make their head spin is not flying drones or smartphone screens. They would be shocked by the pace at which our world exchanges information. Over the past hundred years, news has turned from a sedate, deep stream into a rapid, unceasing tsunami. And this transition from the printing press to instant push notifications has changed more than just media consumption—it has rewired our consciousness.

## The Paper Era: Time to Think

A century ago, a piece of news was an event that required a ritual. A newspaper was published once a day, or even once a week. Information crossed distances over days, weeks, and sometimes even months. This "slow" reporting had one enormous advantage—**time for reflection**. By the time the news reached the editorial office, there was time to fact-check it. While the issue was being laid out, journalists had time to add context and analytics. The reader received not just a bare fact ("something happened"), but a complete story with causes and predicted consequences. The news smelled of printer's ink, it was read over breakfast, discussed for hours, and remained relevant for a long time. Information was a valuable, tangible resource.

## The Era of Live Broadcasts: The Illusion of Presence

The second half of the 20th century brought radio and television, and then the concept of "live broadcasting." The world shrank to the size of a TV screen. The generation that caught the moon landing or the fall of the Berlin Wall live on air felt for the first time: history is being made right now, before our eyes. This generated an incredible level of empathy. But it was exactly then that speed began to slowly push out depth. Why wait for tomorrow's analytical article if you can see the picture in real time?

## The Era of Push Notifications: News as a Dopamine Flash

Today, in 2026, we have reached the absolute peak (or bottom, depending on how you look at it) of this evolution. News is no longer a text or a TV broadcast. It has become a push notification—a line of text on a locked phone screen that vibrates in your pocket a second after something has happened somewhere on the other side of the planet.

> Breaking: Stocks are falling / A rocket has been launched / A star has announced a divorce.

We no longer search for news—it attacks us itself. Live broadcasting is no longer conducted by professional TV companies with outside broadcasting vans, but by any eyewitness with a smartphone via a social media stream. This superspeed has its own, rather high price:

1. **The collapse of verification:** In the rush to "push" the news first, media often sacrifice the truth. No one reads the retractions later.
2. **FOMO syndrome (fear of missing out):** We are constantly refreshing our feeds, afraid of falling behind the agenda by even half an hour.
3. **Clip thinking and "information obesity":** Our brains are not designed to empathize with thirty tragedies and fifty sensational stories a day. The result is emotional burnout and an inability to concentrate on long texts.

The paradox is that, by knowing about everything "right now," we sometimes understand what is happening less than our great-grandparents who read a single newspaper by the fireplace. We know **what** happened, but we do not have the time to figure out **why**.

## Final Thoughts

The speed of news will not increase anymore—there is simply no room to grow; information is transmitted at the speed of light directly to our devices. And today the pendulum is beginning to swing back. If a hundred years ago fresh and fast information was a luxury, today luxury is a **digital detox** and the ability to not know about an event in the very first second. The ability to slow down, turn off push notifications, and wait for a thoughtful long-read instead of endless scrolling through "breaking news" is becoming the main skill of a modern person's information hygiene.
