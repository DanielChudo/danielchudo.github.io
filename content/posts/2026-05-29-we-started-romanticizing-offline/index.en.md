Right before our eyes, a curious cultural shift has occurred. People are once again drawn to the technologies of the past: iPods, PSPs, CD players, and dumbphones. Once, these devices were considered the pinnacle of digital progress. Today, they are perceived almost as analog artifacts. But the word "analog" is no longer about the type of signal. Now it means something entirely different:

- The absence of algorithms and endless feeds
- A device with a single, clear function
- Tactile controls
- Mindful usage
- Offline experience
- A sense of control over one's own time and attention

It's no longer just about nostalgia for the 2000s. It is a reaction to the internet, which has gradually evolved from a tool into an environment of constant consumption. Modern platforms no longer compete for the quality of the experience—they compete for the amount of time spent on them.

We increasingly want to own our own data, to choose our music, movies, and information independently, rather than receiving an endlessly personalized stream of recommendations. Analog culture—in the new understanding of the word—provides a sense of autonomy. Sir Tim Berners-Lee wrote:

> I saw this happen on platforms like Twitter and Facebook, which were optimizing for engagement, rather than enjoyment. It turns out that if you feed people provocative content, you can keep them on your platform for longer. So the AIs started showing nastier and nastier stuff—‘rage bait’, basically—which made users angry and argumentative, but kept them logging on.

And this is indeed the logic of the modern platform economy. The longer a user stays inside a service, the more ads they can be shown, the more data can be collected, and the more accurately algorithms can be tuned. Therefore, it is often more profitable for algorithmic feeds to induce anxiety, irritation, or addiction than to simply show posts from friends or something that makes a person calmer and happier.

Hence the new appeal of old devices. An MP3 player does not try to trap you inside an ecosystem. A push-button phone doesn't turn every free minute into endless scrolling. A film camera does not require you to immediately post a picture on social media for someone else's reaction. These things bring back a rare sense of completion: you turn the device on for a specific action—and turn it off when you're done.

Undoubtedly, part of this phenomenon relies on romanticized nostalgia. People buy Polaroids to take a couple of shots and hang them on the fridge. According to Luminate, about half of the people who bought vinyl records in 2023 do not even own a record player. For many, it is more of an aesthetic object than an actual format for consuming music.

But behind this trend lies a very conscious fatigue. Fatigue from constant connectivity, notifications, endless novelty, and the need to be online all the time. It is especially interesting to observe how young people who have never lived without the internet are joining this movement. We grew up within a digital environment and, for the first time, are starting to perceive disconnecting not as a loss, but as a privilege.

I was skeptical about this for a long time. I am practically always online. The internet shaped me, gave me a social circle, interests, and a job. It became part of my identity. But that is exactly why it is especially noticeable how good it feels when you manage to drop out of the digital stream, even if just for a short while.

This happens during travels, when the phone stops being the center of attention. When you are cooking and not checking notifications every five minutes. When you read a book, play board games, or simply talk to people without the desire to simultaneously photograph, post, or comment on something.

At some point, you begin to realize: the digital world was created as an addition to reality, not a replacement for it. But imperceptibly to ourselves, we began spending too much of our lives inside an endless stream of screens, recommendations, and algorithms—in a space that feels real, but actually exists only to hold our attention for as long as possible.
