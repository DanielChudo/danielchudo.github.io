If there is any topic that interests me almost as much as programming, it is finance. I find it very difficult to keep my mouth shut when this subject comes up. I’m very happy that my tenth post is going to be about money.

On July 31, I am supposed to fly to London. This is a trip I have been planning for a long time. I have big plans for this city: Warner Bros. Studio, the play "Harry Potter and the Cursed Child", the show "Now You See Me", Michelin-starred restaurants, circus cabaret, and much more. But now I have a suspicion that the whole thing might get canceled...

Given the tensions between the countries, there is a small chance that Israeli airspace will be closed and I won’t be able to fly. In that case, my bookings and tickets will go to waste. My first thought was to buy insurance. I had never done that before. That’s how I learned about the **known circumstances exclusion**. An insurer does not cover losses caused by events or circumstances that the policyholder was already aware of before the policy came into effect. You cannot insure a store that has already caught fire. The same applies here: you cannot insure yourself against the risk of a flight cancellation when all the news is reporting a resumption of hostilities.

Maybe I should have been smarter and bought insurance in advance? Nope. All major Israeli insurance companies exclude trip cancellation due to war from their standard policies. The reason is simple: war is a systemic risk. If the conflict escalates, thousands of customers will file claims simultaneously, and the insurer will not be able to compensate everyone for their losses at the regular price of the policy.

While browsing insurers’ websites, I remembered an advertisement I had seen in Tel Aviv. It encouraged people to become their own bank. Behind that slogan is P2P lending. It is a mechanism that allows you to lend money to businesses and potentially earn a higher annual return by acting as the lender. I’m not particularly fond of this way of investing, but I really like their slogan. It makes it clear that you are the boss and you are in control. And I thought: can I apply this approach to my situation? Can I become my own insurer? Or, more precisely, can I **hedge my risks** myself?

Risk hedging is a way to protect your finances from potential market losses by opening opposing positions. In simple terms, it is about creating a “safety net”: if one of your assets falls in value, another one rises and offsets the losses. In my case, the asset is my trip to London. It includes all the money I have spent on Airbnb and concerts. The opposing position is the cancellation of my flight. All that remained was to somehow put money on the possibility that Israeli airspace would be closed on July 31.

Betting on real-world events? Hmm... where have I heard that before? This train of thought led me to Polymarket. Naturally, there was a contract exactly on this event. If you somehow slept through the entire past year and don’t know what this website is, here’s a quick introduction:

Polymarket is a decentralized prediction market platform where people trade not stocks or cryptocurrencies, but probabilities of future events. The questions themselves are very simple. For example: “Will inflation exceed 4% in 2026?” Such questions usually have two outcomes: `YES` and `NO`. But there are also more complicated questions with multiple outcomes (“Which football team will win the World Cup?”) or questions where you have to predict a number (“What will the temperature be at the end of the month?”). Let’s get back to the basic bets.

If you believe an event will happen, you buy `YES`. If you believe it won’t, you buy `NO`. The price of an outcome reflects its probability in real time. If `YES` costs 72 cents, the market believes there is a 72% chance of the event happening. The price is formed in the same way as on a stock exchange and is subject to the classic forces of supply and demand. If you bought `YES` for 40 cents and it later rises to 60 cents, you have two options. First, you can sell `YES` to another user and lock in a 20-cent profit. Second, you can wait until the market resolves and potentially receive $1 if your position wins.

At the time of writing, `YES` in my contract (“Will Israel close its airspace by July 31?”) was priced at 8 cents. This means that for just 8% of the cost of my trip, I can fully hedge this risk. If the trip is canceled because the airspace is closed, the profit from the contract will compensate for my expenses. The only loss will be the cost of the hedge itself — 8% of the total amount. The future is here, old man.

Of course, I wasn’t the only one to come up with this way of using prediction markets. The most prominent example was the collaboration between craft cocktail bar The Jeffrey and Kalshi. The bar promised to cover a bill of up to $100 per person if the Knicks won Game 1 of the NBA Finals. To protect itself against potential losses, the bar placed a $5,000 bet on the Knicks. If they won, the winnings from Kalshi would cover the cost of the free cocktails. If the Knicks lost, the $5,000 loss would be offset by orders placed during the game night. Both scenarios cancel each other out.

There are many things I don’t like about what prediction markets are bringing into our lives, but hedging real-life risks is one of the few positive applications of this technology. Especially in situations where no other reliable institution is willing to compensate you for your losses. For the future, I have come up with a foolproof plan to maximize my protection as a traveler:

1. Fly with Israeli airlines. They always operate until the last possible moment and are required to refund your money if the flight is canceled.
2. Book a hotel or Airbnb with free cancellation. This significantly reduces the share of non-refundable losses.
3. Buy standard insurance for cancellations unrelated to war.
4. Use Polymarket for whatever amount remains unprotected after the previous steps.
