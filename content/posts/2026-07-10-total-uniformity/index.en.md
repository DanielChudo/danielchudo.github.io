Programming has always fascinated me not only because of mathematically elegant algorithms ([RSA](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem)>), [PageRank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PageRank)), but also because it allows me to express my ideas on an electronic "canvas". Web design is an art form, just like painting or sculpting with clay. At least, it used to be.

Sometimes I feel like I arrived too late to the party that was the early internet of the 2000s. Every website had its own personality. Some people remember that era with frustration. Flashing GIFs were everywhere, browser tabs would suddenly start playing music, and every website had its own navigation that you had to figure out from scratch. But that was exactly what made it special. Websites reflected the personality of their creators. They told a story.

Today, mainstream commercial design has become far more cautious and sterile. You'd never be able to tell screenshots from different social networks or messaging apps apart. Everyone copied everyone else for so long that they gradually lost their identity and merged into one homogeneous gray mass.

And that brings us to the central idea of the modern web. A website used to be a work of art; today it's a product. Creators once asked themselves, _"What do I want to say?"_. Now the question is, _"How do we increase user retention?"_

The explanation is largely economic. Design didn't become uniform because designers forgot how to create. It became uniform because the cost of making a mistake has grown astronomically. Designers no longer create simple websites—they design products worth billions of dollars. A single experiment might reduce conversions, increase maintenance costs, hurt accessibility, or require millions of users to relearn familiar interactions. So companies naturally choose the safest path.

For me, the most bittersweet innovation was the rise of UI libraries. As a developer, I felt enormous relief. Every new project no longer required writing `reset.css`, inventing button styles, building navigation menus, and creating grid systems from scratch. UI libraries offered all of that out of the box.

But then came the global Bootstrap pandemic. Ironically, Bootstrap was never intended to become a universal design standard. It started as an internal project at Twitter for a very practical reason: every team built interfaces differently, making the codebase increasingly difficult to maintain. Mark Otto and Jacob Thornton created a shared collection of UI components, and after they open-sourced it on GitHub, it quickly became the world's most popular open-source UI framework.

Each major release felt revolutionary. Bootstrap 2 dramatically simplified responsive design. Bootstrap 3 embraced the mobile-first approach. Soon, there was no escaping the iconic blue rounded Bootstrap button. According to W3Techs, even more than a decade later Bootstrap is still used by roughly one in five websites on the internet. Developers kept reusing the same components and design patterns. Remove the logo, and it was sometimes impossible to tell which website you were looking at. That's how the famous meme was born: _"All Bootstrap websites look the same."_ The problem wasn't Bootstrap itself. It was always meant to be customized. The framework never prevented developers from changing colors, typography, spacing, or layouts. The real problem was that most developers didn't change anything at all.

After 2017, the trend didn't really change. Bootstrap simply gave way to a new design language: Google's Material Design. It was impossible to miss. Rich blue paired with vibrant pink accent colors? That was Material. Borders practically disappeared. In the new paradigm, elements were separated by subtle shadows and generous amounts of whitespace. A floating circular action button occupied the corner of the screen, and every click produced the now-iconic ripple animation. Nearly every component came with this effect by default. And who could forget the Floating Label, where the placeholder gracefully slid upward and shrank as soon as you focused the input field?

For a long time, I couldn't understand why this particular design language won. But the reasons are actually quite rational. It won because it was cheaper and more predictable. Another important factor was the [mere-exposure effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect): the more often people encountered this visual style, the more they tended to like it. Or perhaps... the other way around.

Then, in 2025, the third wave began. Now you can spot AI-generated design from a mile away. If a website was created with ChatGPT, chances are it will feature oversized headlines, an almost monochromatic color palette (except for a purple gradient button and a few blurry glowing blobs), no icons, extremely short text, and a layout where nearly every section is presented as a card. Claude, on the other hand, has a noticeably warmer aesthetic: cream tones, beige backgrounds, brown accents, and a signature serif typeface—a refreshing contrast to the sans-serif typography that has dominated the tech industry for the past two decades.

But it's important to understand one thing: AI didn't invent this style. It averaged it. Large language models were trained on thousands of modern landing pages, so they naturally reproduce the statistically most likely design. ChatGPT isn't making websites look the same—it is amplifying a trend that already existed.

As our phones became flatter, so did our interfaces. Many people criticize Apple's new Liquid Glass design language. But at least it feels alive. It plays with light, depth, and your attention. When touchscreens first appeared, the original iPhone embraced [skeuomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeuomorph) to restore the sense of physicality that glass screens had taken away (the camera shutter clicked like a real camera, and the Notes app looked like textured paper). Today, respect for the visual language of real-world objects survives mostly in music production software.

Platforms have brought standardization. You no longer need to learn the interface of every individual blog. Instead, we've become dependent on the decisions of a small group of product designers. Any attempt at individuality is often rejected by product managers who are afraid to experiment. And yet experimentation is exactly how breakthroughs happen. You never know what idea will resonate. The only way to find out is to try everything. Some ideas become legendary. Others fail spectacularly. But in the process, you'll truly be ALIVE.

I'm not suggesting we bring back the eye-searing custom CSS of MySpace. But people have always wanted to express who they are. That's why they carefully design their YouTube channel banners, customize their Instagram Highlights covers, or add unique emoji next to their names on Telegram. Even tiny personal touches help make a digital space feel like it truly belongs to you.

I sometimes spend hours browsing old web designs on Pinterest. The sheer density of visual details is incredible. Modern mainstream interfaces have become so smooth and so afraid of saying anything that your eyes simply glide over them without finding anything memorable. They've become incredibly usable, yet remarkably bland. It often feels as though dozens of completely different products were designed by the same person using the same template.

Minimalism is wonderful when it removes unnecessary complexity. But it stops being a virtue when it removes personality along with the clutter. Design isn't just about solving problems. It's also about communicating emotion, character, and values. Maybe that's why so many people still feel nostalgic for the early web. Not because it was easier to use, but because it felt alive—full of courage, imperfections, and a willingness to experiment. Sometimes the results were terrible, but they were almost never forgettable.
