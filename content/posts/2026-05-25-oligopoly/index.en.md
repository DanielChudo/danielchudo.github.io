When you're about to listen to music, the thought hardly crosses your mind: "Hmm, is this Taylor Swift track available on Spotify, or will I have to go to Apple Music?" But with movies and TV shows, the rules of the game change radically. Any recommendation from a friend is inevitably accompanied by a clarifying question: "Is it on Netflix?" A natural question arises: why does music streaming work seamlessly, while video platforms have turned into a fragmented obstacle course?

The fundamental reason lies in the structure of copyright ownership. In the music market, the "Big Three" reigns supreme: Universal, Sony, and Warner. Together, they control about **70% of all commercial music on the planet**. Streaming services only need to sign licensing agreements with just three giants to satisfy the needs of most music lovers. Of course, such an oligopoly did not form overnight—it is the result of decades of aggressive mergers, acquisitions, and bankruptcies of independent labels.

At first glance, a similar pattern can be observed in the film industry. The "Big Five" rules the roost here: Disney, Warner Bros., Universal, Sony, and Paramount, accumulating roughly the same **70% of the global box office**. However, the similarities end there. While music labels and streaming services are separate entities operating on a **many-to-many** model (everyone licenses content to everyone), in the video market, this architecture falls apart. Here, the rightsholder and the distribution platform are one and the same. The industry has built closed ecosystems: Disney has Disney+, Warner Bros. has HBO, Paramount has Paramount+. Tech giants are not lagging behind either. In 2022, Amazon swallowed the historic Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer studio (the one with the roaring lion in the intro), integrating the rights to the James Bond franchise and an impressive library of classics into its Prime Video.

Record companies absolutely do not care through which "pipeline" their royalties flow. Therefore, music services compete at the product level: UX/UI design, predictive algorithms, and audio quality. For film studios, however, the math is different: showing content on their own platform allows them to keep all the profits, bypassing intermediary commission fees. Realizing this, media giants launched a process of fragmentation, revoking the rights to their flagship projects from third-party services in order to herd audiences into their own apps. A paradoxical situation arose: while we choose a music service for its smart algorithms, we choose a video service solely for its exclusive library. And if your ideal weekend involves "Stranger Things" for breakfast, "The Boys" for lunch, and "The Mandalorian" for dinner—get ready to pay for three different subscriptions.

This whole situation looks like bitter irony when you recall the visionary origins of the streaming king—Netflix. In the early 2000s, the market was a chaotic mess of competing studios and cable networks: you had to pay separately for every channel, and movies had to be purchased on physical media. Netflix sparked a revolution, first by aggregating all content via its DVD mail-delivery service, and then within a unified streaming interface. This was the golden era of streaming video, which ended the exact moment traditional rightsholders realized the true value of their assets in the internet age. The "Streaming Wars" began. After all, how do you poach a subscriber if all platforms have an identical catalog? And although Netflix initially built its empire on a brilliant recommendation system, any algorithm, no matter how advanced, is useless if the desired show is physically absent from the servers. Competitors started striking at the most vulnerable spot: they pulled their hits from opponents' libraries and began pouring billions into exclusive content. The strategy worked. Today, the world is ruled by FOMO (fear of missing out): want to stay in the cultural loop and discuss the finale of "Stranger Things"? You have no choice but to subscribe to Netflix.

Ironically, the industry has come full circle and returned to its monopolistic roots. Up until the mid-20th century, Hollywood majors controlled the entire film production chain: they produced the content, owned the theater chains, and aggressively forced package deals (block booking) onto independent exhibitors. In business terminology, this is classic **vertical integration**. This corporate dictate would have continued forever if U.S. antitrust regulators had not intervened. Independent venues were suffocating without access to blockbusters, studios forced them to buy flop releases bundled with the hits, and market competition was trending toward zero. The culmination arrived in 1948, when the Supreme Court handed down a historic decision in a landmark case: "United States v. Paramount Pictures".

The court's verdict was harsh: movie studios were banned from owning theaters, enforcing block booking, and dictating distribution terms. The forced sell-off of theatrical assets forever changed the market landscape, giving rise to modern Hollywood—an industry where studios had to bet on product quality rather than a monopoly on distribution. But this status quo lasted only until 2019, when the U.S. Department of Justice made a resonant statement:

> The restrictions were created for the 1940s market and no longer correspond to the realities of the modern industry.

In 2020, a federal court officially terminated the historic decrees. If, in the analog paradigm, a major studio reserved the best physical theaters exclusively for its own releases, then in the new system streaming services artificially boost algorithmic traffic and prime homepage placement for their own content. Music platforms have also adopted this gray tactic: they increasingly integrate their own music into top playlists to avoid paying commissions to major labels. Lately, they have actively been bringing in AI song generation to scale this process. Ultimately, the entertainment industry quietly mutated and reverted to the very fragmented state that Netflix once promised to save viewers from...
