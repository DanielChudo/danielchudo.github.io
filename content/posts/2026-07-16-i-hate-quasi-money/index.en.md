People say that cash is a bad gift. Supposedly, it means you didn't put in any effort and couldn't be bothered to spend time choosing something personal. I completely disagree—at least when we're not talking about close friends or family. I'd much rather receive cash and buy something I actually want than get a present I'll never use. There is, however, one form of money that I absolutely hate: **quasi-money**.

In economics, quasi-money refers to highly liquid financial assets that aren't money in the strict sense—you can't walk into a bakery and buy a loaf of bread with them—but can quickly be converted into cash. Bank deposits, savings accounts, government bonds, and similar instruments all fall into this category. I have no problem with those. The problem is what the term has come to mean today.

To me, quasi-money is anything companies give you instead of real money. Reward points, airline miles, store credits, loyalty programs—they're all private monetary substitutes, entirely controlled by the company that issued them and designed to lock you into its ecosystem. Reward points can only be spent at a specific chain of stores, while airline miles are only valid with a handful of airlines. They're not universal, and they encourage you to shop where it may not actually be in your best interest.

Quasi-money has another major drawback that real money doesn't: expiration dates. In most cases, these assets simply disappear if you don't use them before a certain deadline. Sometimes you get a year. Sometimes only a month. That's how companies erase their liabilities and make money in the process. The industry even has a name for it: **breakage**—the value of rewards that are never redeemed.

Imagine someone gives you a $100 gift card for your birthday. You spend $92 and leave the remaining $8 on the card forever. Those $8 become pure profit for the company. According to industry estimates, ~~76% of all statistics are made up~~ somewhere between 15% and 40% of all reward points are never redeemed. There are countless reasons why. Companies have millions of abandoned loyalty accounts. People stop visiting a coffee chain, switch banks, delete an app, lose a gift card, or simply don't fly for a couple of years.

And that's before we even get to the fine print. A "free" airline ticket might cost 500,000 miles—but only on Tuesdays, with no checked baggage. Technically, the redemption option exists. In practice, it doesn't.

Things got so far that, back in 2014, people on the internet started calling Starbucks a bank (around the same time, there was another popular argument claiming that McDonald's was actually a real estate company—but that's a story for another post). It all started when Starbucks introduced the ability to preload money into its mobile app. At that moment, you still haven't received your coffee, but Starbucks has already received your money. That money represents a liability: Starbucks owes you a future cup of coffee. Until then, however, it gets to use your cash. In other words, you just gave Starbucks an **interest-free loan**. According to the company's financial reports, by the spring of 2025 Starbucks was holding nearly $2 billion in customer balances through gift cards and loyalty programs. That very breakage.

So why would customers agree to this? Because Starbucks offered a compelling deal: extra reward points, one-tap payments (much more valuable in the early 2010s than they are today, when Google Pay is everywhere), and personalized offers.

There was another benefit—for Starbucks. Every time you pay with Visa or Mastercard, the merchant has to pay fees to both the card network and the acquiring bank. But when you buy coffee using your Starbucks app balance, no money actually moves anywhere. Internally, it's just a number being subtracted from one database entry and added to another. The only real card transaction happened when you topped up your balance. Whether you buy one latte or ten afterward, Starbucks doesn't have to pay transaction fees to a third party.

Speaking of lattes, how much does a cup cost? A few years ago, you could redeem 150 Stars for a latte. Today, the price is 200 Stars. Starbucks changed the redemption rate unilaterally, effectively reducing the value of the rewards people had already earned. So not only did you give the company an interest-free loan—you also watched the purchasing power of that loan shrink.

This entire arrangement shows how a retailer can effectively become a fintech company without a banking license or banking regulation. A real bank is required to keep a portion of customer deposits in reserve in case of a bank run. In theory, Starbucks can put 100% of those prepaid funds to work—expanding its store network or funding marketing campaigns—and if the equivalent of a bank run ever occurred, it could simply devalue Stars, making its obligations cheaper to fulfill—that is, cheaper to sell you coffee.

Today, quasi-money is everywhere.Try adding up all the forgotten value you've accumulated over the years: gift cards from work, cashback that came back as store points instead of cash, airline miles you'll never use, loyalty balances scattered across dozens of apps. Every one of those balances represents money you could have spent however you wanted—if it had simply been deposited into your bank account instead. We've deliberately narrowed the usefulness of real money to create an illusion of generosity and personalization

The only real advantage I've been able to find is that, for some people, this kind of gift may actually work better than giving cash. Google tells me there's a name for this: **soft paternalism**. It's the idea of gently nudging people toward what someone else believes is a better choice. Maybe you're worried that a person would spend cash on unhealthy habits. Or maybe they're so frugal that they would never buy themselves anything nice—not even on their birthday. Then, when receiving points, those psychological barriers will disappear (which can be a very bad thing in some situations: paying with a single tap instead of with crinkling banknotes and clinking coins has removed the psychological burden and sense of guilt associated with spending, contributing to the rise of a consumer society). Everyone has to decide for themselves whether they are a controlling parent who believes they know what is best for how others should live.
